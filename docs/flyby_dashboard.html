Where gravity assists do work for this architecture

The probe in this series cruises at ~450 km/s — slow enough that a close pass by a solar-mass star deflects its path by a few degrees. More importantly, a powered burn at closest approach (the Oberth maneuver) is the primary launch mechanism from Earth's solar system: dive to 0.01–0.02 AU, fire the drive at perihelion, and the depth in the gravity well multiplies every unit of propellant. The vehicle paper uses this as the baseline Earth departure strategy. At 450 km/s the Sun's escape velocity (~618 km/s at the surface) is comparable to cruise speed, so gravity assists remain useful for steering and launch — just not at relativistic cruise.

Where they fail

Above a few percent of lightspeed, the focal point of a 90° gravitational turn falls inside every ordinary star — the geometry becomes impossible. White dwarfs help slightly (smaller radius, same mass); only neutron stars can turn a relativistic probe, and none lie within 100 light-years of Earth. The scoop is similarly limited: close passes yield grams of bulk hydrogen per pass, not fusion fuel, and the heat flux at 0.1 AU during a 10%-of-c flyby exceeds 10 MW/m² — enough to destroy any practical collector.
