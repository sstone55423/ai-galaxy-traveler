# Founded from Earth: A Home-System Programme for the Slow Lineage

**S. Stone**
*Working draft, revision 1. Eighteenth in a series on a slow, self-replicating interstellar AI probe. The roadmap paper ("Settlement Zero") lays the on-ramp from today's capability to the lineage's first dispatch and, at its second revision, had grown a supply track, an interstellar-object rider, and a fuel budget that were about the home system's industrial programme rather than about validation. This paper takes those over, adds the launch platform, the scouts, the comet rider, and the first probes outward, and asks the one question the on-ramp could defer: what leaves the solar system before settlement zero exists.*

---

## Abstract

The roadmap paper's readiness ladder is a sequence of demonstrators under a no-resupply rule, and a demonstrator is the wrong instrument for everything the lineage needs in quantity. This paper describes the programme around the ladder: three tracks beside the ladder, run in parallel on Earth and the Moon ahead of settlement zero, that meet the ladder only at the declared inventory. A supply track mines, purifies, and banks fuel, the Moon's Procellarum KREEP Terrane first, because chondritic rock at parts-per-billion uranium cannot supply U-235 by mining and can supply fissile material only by breeding from thorium and U-238. A launch platform, a lunar mass driver of 50 to 500 km for tens of km/s, does what no Earth launch does cheaply: it puts prospectors in the belt and beyond, riders on passing interstellar objects, and instruments on recurring comets. A scouting track details our own solar system, body by body, as opposed to the sky, and gives the supply track its assay and the ladder its feedstock. And a first handful of probes goes outward before any off-Earth fuel exists. The interstellar-object rider, one resident and four held seeds, senses the heliosheath and the Oort cloud for the ~8,000 years its reactor sustains instruments, keeps a watt-level vigil after, and, provisioned with two to four charges' worth of fuel per seed, reaches stellar passes at 1 to 2 pc, yielding of order 0.8 expected settlements per rider. The comet rider, serviced every perihelion, is its rehearsal. The hard question is what an early rider may carry. Under the series' no-recall decision a reproduction-capable seed beyond the heliopause is the lineage's first irreversible act, so early riders are observatories and dormancy testbeds with inert seed hardware, confined to the early phase, and the seeds that can found a lineage ride only after the standing to launch has been exercised. The programme's limits are governance and time, not physics: the fissile chain is a dual-use problem by the hundred kilograms, the platform is a weapon by construction, and the political window the subsystem budget paper measures in decades must be outlived by a platform that does not need it.

---

## 1. The Programme the Ladder Needs

The roadmap paper renders the road from today's capability to the lineage's first dispatch as a readiness ladder, S0 through S5: the capability inventory, a terrestrial closed-loop plant, an off-Earth process demonstrator, a near-Earth asteroid seed, settlement zero in the main belt, and first dispatch. Every rung above S0 runs under a no-resupply rule, because the quantity the series most needs measured, the vitamin fraction, is by definition what a settlement cannot make, and a demonstrator that may be resupplied measures nothing. That rule is the ladder's virtue and its limit. A demonstrator forbidden to receive anything cannot be the thing that supplies fuel by the tonne, launches prospectors by the dozen, or puts a rider on a body that passes once. Those are a programme's work, and this paper is about the programme.

It has four tracks, and they run at once. The ladder itself, kept in the roadmap paper. A supply track that mines, purifies, and banks fuel and feedstock (Section 3). A launch platform on the Moon (Section 4). And a scouting track that details the solar system body by body (Section 5). The three tracks described here are exempt from the no-resupply rule, may be human-serviced, and are judged on what they deliver; they meet the ladder at one place only, the declared inventory a seed carries when it is delivered, so that nothing they do corrupts the measurement the ladder exists to make. The programme's product is settlement zero provisioned, a platform that outlives the programme, and a first handful of probes outward, the interstellar-object rider (Section 6), rehearsed on recurring comets (Section 7). What those first probes may carry is the programme's one hard question (Section 8), and its governance (Section 9), ordering (Section 10), and failure modes (Section 11) follow from the answer. Throughout, the roadmap paper's rung labels S0 to S5 and its vocabulary of seed, child, sub-probe, and prospector are used unchanged; rider and resident are defined in Section 6, and scout, an Earth-built craft doing the prospector's job before any settlement can build one, in Section 5. Two launchers appear, and they are never the same thing: the lunar driver of Section 4, for tens of km/s, and settlement zero's launcher, the roadmap paper's thousand-kilometre driver for cruise speed.

## 2. What Earth Has

The programme starts with more than the ladder assumes, and it is worth stating the inventory as numbers, because none of them binds.

Fissile material. About 1,250 tonnes of highly enriched uranium exist (International Panel on Fissile Materials 2022). The lineage's first generation, a dozen seeds at ~30 kg (charge plus cruise fuel) and a few riders at ~250 kg, is of order a tonne, under a tenth of a percent of that stock. Enrichment and breeding capacity exist at industrial scale on Earth and can be built on the Moon. The constraint on fissile material in the Earth era is who may hold it and where it may go, not whether it exists.

The Moon, as a platform and as a source. It sits in a 2.4 km/s well with no atmosphere, three days away, which is what makes a mass driver there the launch platform of Section 4. It is also a fuel source in its own right. The Procellarum KREEP Terrane carries thorium at ~10 ppm, several hundred times chondrite (Section 3). The polar cold traps hold surface-exposed water ice (Li et al. 2018) for propellant and chemistry. And the regolith holds solar-wind helium-3, of order 10^9 kg of it (Wittenberg et al. 1986), which the lineage does not run on, since its reactors burn U-235, but which the supply track banks against a fusion capability the series assumes may come later (Section 3).

Launch and cadence. Heavy lift exists at a cadence of many flights a year to orbit and of a few a year to escape, enough to deliver a 3,700 kg seed to a belt body, the roadmap paper's Earth-to-belt mission, and to build a lunar driver segment by segment. What Earth's rockets do badly is the rest: tens of km/s for an eighteen-tonne rider, again and again, which is the lunar driver's job.

Demonstrated capability. The roadmap paper's S0 inventory holds: sample return from carbonaceous bodies, in-situ resource use on Mars, a ground-tested kilowatt fission reactor, a flight-tested autonomous planner, and printers that print most of their own parts. Robotic mining at ppb grades is chemistry known from seawater and not yet done on rock off Earth; that is the supply track's first experiment.

The point of the inventory is the sentence it supports: no physical limit binds the Earth era. Its limits are governance and time, and the rest of this paper is about spending both well.

## 3. The Supply Track

The ladder is a sequence of demonstrators, and demonstrators under the no-resupply rule are the wrong instrument for one thing the ladder needs in quantity: fuel. The fissile vitamin is the one of the bootstrapping paper's three bottlenecks that the ladder needs by the hundred kilograms, and Section 6 shows that a single provisioned rider wants of order 250 kg of U-235. That material comes from a parallel supply track, distinct from the ladder, whose product is stored fuel and assay data rather than closure. Supply sites are exempt from the no-resupply rule, as Section 1 says, and are judged on tonnes delivered. The two tracks meet at the declared inventory. Fissile delivered to an S3 or S4 seed is declared inventory and counted as vitamin, so the closure measurement is unaffected and simply records fissile as un-closed, which is what the bootstrapping paper predicts. A feedstock depot is different: it is not drawn on until the closure levels it would mask have been measured, and what is drawn afterward is logged as supplement, never as closure. The track's sites, in the order the geology and the delta-v rank them:

- *The Moon, first.* The Procellarum KREEP Terrane (Jolliff et al. 2000) carries thorium reaching ~10 ppm and locally more (Lawrence et al. 1998, 2000), with uranium at the chondritic Th/U ratio of ~3.7 (Lodders 2003), about a quarter of that: the richest concentration of either element known off Earth, several hundred times chondrite, three days away in a 2.4 km/s well with no radiation belts. A lunar mine with a breeder is the fissile chain of the whole home-system era: U-235 for reactors, U-233 bred from thorium for the vigil batteries of Section 6, and neptunium-237, a scarce by-product, as that section's scale marker. Fissile material stores for any programmatic timescale, U-233 behind the shielding its U-232 contamination demands, so the mine can start before the ladder does and bank. It is also the programmatic reason for choosing the Moon at the roadmap paper's S2 rung.
- *The belt, second.* Ceres for water, carbonaceous bodies for volatiles, metal bodies for structure: settlement zero's own feedstock, cached before the S4 seed arrives so that it lands beside a depot.
- *Mars and the Jupiter Trojans, as secondary sources.* The Martian crust carries thorium at 0.2 to 1 ppm (Taylor et al. 2006), of order ten to thirty times chondrite, behind a 5 km/s well that Phobos and Deimos make bearable as depots. Its atmosphere is 1.9% argon (Mahaffy et al. 2013), an electric-propulsion propellant. The Trojans are a second belt at 5.2 AU, of order 10^5 bodies larger than a kilometre (Emery et al. 2015), dark and probably volatile-rich, presumably chondritic at ppb and therefore breeder feedstock, in shallow wells far from Jupiter's radiation.
- *Callisto and Enceladus, as depots and bases, not as suppliers.* Callisto's water ice sits outside Jupiter's worst radiation in a 2.4 km/s well; Enceladus has the smallest well of any icy moon worth the trip and a plume, fed by hydrothermal reaction of water with rock (Waite et al. 2017), that a spacecraft samples by flying through it. Titan's nitrogen and methane (Niemann et al. 2005) are carbon and nitrogen feedstock under a thick, cold atmosphere. None has accessible uranium at first order, since their rock lies under ice, and hauling volatiles inward from 5 to 10 AU costs years and several km/s against water that Ceres already offers. Their value to the lineage is as outer-system sites: prospector targets, cold-archive sites, and rehearsal for operating at 1 to 4% sunlight on fission alone, the regime of every seed's approach and of any dim host.

*Solar-wind volatiles, and helium-3.* Every regolith-processing site in the track heats regolith, for water and volatiles, and heating releases the solar-wind gases implanted in it: hydrogen, helium-4, nitrogen, carbon, and helium-3 at of order 10 ppb. The track's rule is that every mining, refining, and storage site captures and banks them, helium-3 included, as a by-product of work already being done; the KREEP mine that yields one rider's 250 kg of U-235 processes ~1.2 × 10^10 kg of regolith and so yields ~120 kg of helium-3 for the cost of a cold trap and a tank. The reason is an assumption this series states and isolates: that a fusion capability may become available to the lineage at some later time, and that nothing in the architecture depends on it. The energy is real, since deuterium-helium-3 fusion releases ~6 × 10^14 J per kilogram of helium-3, so a kilogram is worth roughly eight of the seed's 16.5 kg fission charges at comparable conversion; the reactor is not, and the banked stock is an option, not a budget line. Outside fusion, helium-3 is not a fuel in any form, being inert and not fissile, but the bank has present value that does not wait on the assumption: helium-3 gas is the standard thermal-neutron detector, which the seed's reactor instrumentation and the neutron assay of thorium and uranium ores both use, and it is the working fluid of the dilution refrigerators that cool detectors to millikelvin. The seed carries none of it in its vitamin set. It carries the extraction know-how, which is mass-free, because helium is a gas whose containment over millennia is an unsolved question, because it buys nothing under the fission design in flight, and because every airless regolith exposed to a stellar wind carries it, so a settlement that needs it, for detectors now or for fusion later, extracts it where it is.

*The state of fusion, 2026.* The assumption should carry its date. As this is written no device has produced more electricity than it consumed, and none runs on deuterium-helium-3. What exists is research at three scales whose durations differ by a factor of a billion. The longest confined plasma is ~22 minutes: 1,337 seconds of hydrogen plasma in the WEST tokamak in February 2025 (CEA 2025), a control result with no meaningful fusion output. The longest fusion burn at scale is ~5 seconds: JET's deuterium-tritium pulses released 59 MJ at a plasma gain of ~0.33 (Maslov et al. 2023), raised to 69 MJ in the final campaign of 2023 (EUROfusion 2024), still below break-even on the plasma alone. The highest gain lasts a nanosecond: the National Ignition Facility's shot of December 2022 produced 3.1 MJ from 2.05 MJ of laser light, a target gain of 1.5 (Abu-Shawareb et al. 2024), and later shots have reached ~8 MJ at gains near four (Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory 2025), at most about one shot a day, with the lasers drawing hundreds of megajoules from the wall for each. Sustained net-energy fusion of any kind has therefore lasted zero seconds. The nearest milestones are a compact high-field tokamak designed for plasma gain above two in ten-second pulses (Creely et al. 2020), with first plasma now expected in 2027; ITER's deuterium-tritium operation, rebaselined to 2039 (ITER Organization 2024); and the one deuterium-helium-3 effort, a pulsed machine that reported measurable deuterium-tritium fusion at 150 million degrees in 2026 with no published net electricity (Helion Energy 2026). Deuterium-helium-3 is itself a step beyond deuterium-tritium, needing several times the temperature for a lower reaction rate (Wittenberg et al. 1986), so even a working deuterium-tritium plant would leave the bank one development away from use. That is why the assumption is stated as it is: fusion is recognised, not available, and the bank waits.

Three rules govern the track. Supply sites are not evidence of closure, and their data feeds the S1 simulant and the prospector census rather than the vitamin trajectory. Fissile accounting is continuous and ledgered from mine to seed, because a lunar enrichment and breeding chain producing hundreds of kilograms of weapons-usable material is the ethics paper's dual-use problem (Section 9), and the roadmap paper's information clause, which the governance paper has since adopted for the home-system case, applies to the depot before it applies to the settlement. And storage is sized for the whole first generation. The seeds and riders are of order a tonne of fissile material (Section 2), plus thousands of tonnes of water banked in the belt. Settlement zero's own reactors are a separate and larger line: at the seed reactor's conversion and burn-up a megawatt-electric consumes ~14 kg of U-235 a year, hundreds of kilograms over the decades of S4, and the hundreds of megawatts a launcher needs would consume tonnes a year, which is why the settlement's industrial power must come from breeding or from arrays before settlement zero's launcher is built. The track is the content of the phrase "founded from Earth": the lineage's first generation leaves on Moon-bred fuel and belt-mined structure.

## 4. The Launch Platform

The roadmap paper sizes the launcher a settlement needs to send a seed to 450 km/s and finds it the largest structure in the architecture, a mass driver of a thousand to ten thousand kilometres. The home-system programme needs a launcher for a different job, and it is two orders of magnitude smaller. Nothing in this paper leaves at cruise speed. Prospectors need a few km/s beyond lunar escape; a rider needs to match a hyperbolic body at tens of km/s; a comet rendezvous is Rosetta-class, a few km/s with gravity assists. The platform for all three is the lunar mass driver O'Neill (1974) proposed for lifting lunar material to escape speed, in the high-acceleration form the 1977 Ames study designed (Kolm et al. 1979), scaled up in exit speed and not in principle.

The numbers are the roadmap paper's formula, L = v²/(2a), at lunar scale. The Ames design, 2.4 km/s at ~1,000 g, is ~300 m long, and its prototypes reached accelerations of that order on the bench. Ten km/s at 1,000 g is ~5 km. Thirty km/s, the rider's rendezvous speed, is ~46 km at 1,000 g and ~460 km at 100 g; the choice between them is whether the payload tolerates 1,000 g, which the roadmap paper leaves open (it rules out 10,000 g for a reactor with moving conversion machinery) and which the S1 plant can test on the ground. Either length is buildable on a body of 7 × 10^22 kg that anchors the recoil for free, and a hundred kilometres is a railway, not a country. The energy is trivial: an 18.5-tonne rider at 30 km/s carries ~8 × 10^12 J, and a launch a year averages ~260 kW, which a square kilometre of lunar array delivers several hundred to a thousand times over. A payload leaving the driver at 30 km/s has ~30 km/s of hyperbolic excess relative to the Moon and, added to or subtracted from Earth's 30 km/s orbital motion, heliocentric speeds from near zero to ~60 km/s, which spans the heliocentric speeds a rendezvous with an inbound interstellar object or an inbound comet asks for. Every number here reproduces from the driver function in engineering.py.

Against that, an Earth launch of the same rider needs a heavy-lift vehicle and a high-energy upper stage for each attempt, pays 11 km/s of well and an atmosphere, and cannot repeat at the cadence interstellar objects arrive. The Moon wins for anything that must leave fast and often, and the platform's three jobs are exactly that. First, scouts: the Earth-built craft of Section 5, launched from the Moon to the belt, the Trojans, and outward to do the prospector's job before any settlement can build one, so that the census runs a decade earlier. Second, riders: the LSST is expected to find one to three interstellar objects a year, and rendezvous-class missions can reach of order one over the survey's decade (Hoover et al. 2022); a driver that can put an eighteen-tonne package on an inbound hyperbolic trajectory within weeks of discovery raises that to perhaps a few, since the binding constraint on rendezvous accessibility is delta-v and response time, both of which the driver supplies. Third, comets: the recurring platforms of Section 7, at Rosetta's cost.

The platform is also the programme's most durable product, and Section 10 says why that matters.

## 5. Solar-System Scouting

The scouts' main function is detailing our own solar system, body by body, as opposed to the sky, which is the work of Hubble- and Webb-class observatories and of surveys such as the LSST. The roadmap paper defines the prospector as the sub-probe variant a settlement builds for its resource census, and places its first flight at S3, the near-Earth seed, as the first product of L3 manufacture. That remains the right test of the settlement. It is the wrong schedule for the census. The census the replication model needs, composition, accessibility, and mass of every body a settlement might use, is also the assay the supply track needs and the feedstock fidelity the S1 plant needs, and none of those should wait a decade for a seed to build its first instrument. The scouting track launches scouts from the lunar platform, from the start, and the S3 seed's own prospector then tests manufacture rather than discovery; its survey confirms an inherited census rather than makes one, which is the L3 test the roadmap paper wants and a weaker test of the knowledge-growth paper's derivation step.

What the scouts measure. In the belt: the metal, silicate, and volatile inventory of candidate settlement-zero bodies, and their uranium and thorium at ppb, which no body beyond Earth has yet been assayed for in situ. At the Trojans and Mars: the secondary sources of Section 3. In the outer system: the depots and rehearsal sites, Callisto's ice, Enceladus's plume, Titan's atmosphere, and the radiation and thermal envelopes of each region, which decide what a rider or a seed must survive there. Along the way: the interplanetary environment, dust and plasma, at the resolution a fleet of small craft gives and a single flagship does not. The outer-system scouts are also the programme's rehearsal for what a seed does on arrival at another star: operating at 1 to 4% sunlight on fission alone, and entering a system from its cold edge inward.

What the scouts are not. They are not observatories. Stellar mapping, exoplanet characterisation, and deep-sky astrometry belong to purpose-built instruments, and the roadmap paper's Section 3 already lets the settlement grow those. A scout's instrument is an assay suite, an imager, and an environment package, sized for a body at a time.

## 6. The Interstellar-Object Rider

The roadmap paper's Section 11 declined the interstellar object as a dispatch and kept it as a node. This section says what the node is, and it is the first thing the launch platform of Section 4 sends outward. A vault with no active systems would waste what the body offers, and the knowledge-growth paper's yardstick says why: a node that can still sense and learn is a node, whatever it is riding, and what counts is knowledge carried forward, not survival. This section states what an active rider is, how long it is one, and what its complement should be. It is also, with the comet rider of Section 7, an instance of the moving resource base the consistency review lists as a candidate paper, a body used as a platform rather than a destination: the comet rider is the first and the one that stays, and the interstellar rider is the one that leaves.

**What the body offers.** Not speed and not direction: the body leaves at its arrival speed on a trajectory chosen by no one. What it offers is mass. A rider buried in a body of order a hundred metres, the scale Meech et al. (2017) infer for 'Oumuamua, has shielding against dust and most cosmic rays that no seed in cruise can carry, thermal mass, an anchor, and, if the body is volatile-rich, propellant for its own small craft and for the late release below. The vehicle paper's cruise-survival figure, 0.99 per light-year at 450 km/s, is a dust, radiation, and ageing figure; a buried rider at 26 km/s faces neither the dust flux nor the impact energy and is shielded from most of the radiation, so its survival is set by ageing alone, for which the earlier papers have no number.

**The active window.** The resident carries the same 16.5 kg fissile charge as any seed. At the 150 W standard profile that is ~8,000 years of instruments, ledger, and beam; the 50 W range-maximizer profile stretches the charge to ~24,000 years but is, in the vehicle paper's definition, a hibernation profile that shuts the instruments and the beam. The rider therefore senses for ~8,000 years and persists for ~24,000, and at 26 km/s those are 0.7 and 2.1 light-years. The interesting fact is where they fall. The body crosses the heliopause, which Voyager 1 found at ~122 AU (Stone et al. 2013), in about twenty years; it passes 1,000 AU in about 180; it enters the inner Oort cloud, which begins at ~2,000 AU, within a few hundred years, and the cloud extends to ~10^5 AU (Dones et al. 2015), which the body reaches after some 18,000. The rider's sensing life is spent entirely inside the solar system's own outermost reservoir, ending ~44,000 AU in; its ledger outlives it to just past the cloud's edge. That is the mission, not a limitation. The heliosheath and the very local interstellar medium are the regions the Voyagers sampled at 17 km/s for a few decades; the Oort cloud no spacecraft has reached. A rider samples all three for eight thousand years and to several hundred times the Voyagers' distance, in situ, with instruments it can point. In the knowledge-growth paper's terms it is a D1 node in the capability sense, sub-probe capable and launcher-less, in motion, growing observational knowledge through what would otherwise be dead time. It is also a kin node of the lineage-network paper's kind, on a budget of its own: 100 W through a 1 m aperture at 1 µm into settlement zero's 100 m receiver delivers of order 10^4 photons per second at 1 ly, a few kilobits per second, and a quarter of that at 2 ly, enough for a ledger and a manifest. Its knowledge reaches Earth through the root for as long as Earth listens.

**After the window.** When the charge is spent the rider is the cold archive the roadmap paper describes, and the question is whether anything wakes it. Sunlight will not: at 10 AU from a star the flux is ~14 W/m², at 100 AU ~0.14 W/m², at 2,000 AU ~3 × 10^-4 W/m², and a body moving at a few tens of km/s relative to the field stars, through a stellar density of ~0.1 per cubic parsec, passes within 0.5 pc of a star once per a few hundred thousand years (~500,000 at the body's own 26 km/s; ~200,000 on the Gaia-measured rate of 20 ± 2 encounters within 1 pc per million years, Bailer-Jones et al. 2018, scaled to 0.5 pc), within 0.1 pc once per ~12 million years, and within 2,000 AU once per ~10^9 years. A photovoltaic trigger never fires. A late action therefore needs a reserved charge, a clock or an astrometric trigger, and a mechanism that still works after 10^5 years dormant, which is the ageing question the earlier papers cannot yet bound. Buried, the rider's odds are better than a seed's in cruise; how much better cannot yet be known.

**Five, not one.** The rider's complement should be five packages: one resident, four held for release. The resident is the node: reactor, instruments, ledger, transmitter, anchored and buried, and it never leaves the body. The four are seeds, mobile bootstrap packages held dormant with reserved charges, and their purpose is the stellar pass: four, so that the first pass can receive the routing paper's three independent attempts and one seed remains for a second. A late release is a dispatch in the routing paper's sense, since it is aimed at a star, and it is the one deliberate exception to the rule that launch impulse comes from settlement-built infrastructure. There is no settlement, so the seed leaves under its own nuclear-electric drive at tens of km/s, on propellant the resident has drawn from the body during the window: a 3,700 kg seed needs a comparable mass of propellant for 30 km/s at an exhaust velocity of 50 km/s, and cannot carry it from the belt. The transit is slow. A pass at 0.5 pc is 1.6 light-years away, which at tens of km/s is ten to twenty thousand years and consumes ~11 kg at the 50 W profile, two-thirds of a charge, on a reactor that has already waited 10^5 years or more; the seed then brakes on the star's wind and interstellar medium as any seed does, more easily at tens of km/s than at 450, since d = m/(2ρA) does not care about speed and the time it takes is time the seed has. What the window buys the four is the one thing a seed in cruise never has: a resident that inspects and repairs them for eight thousand years, running the lower classes of the vehicle paper's repair spectrum, inspection, replacement from spares, and minor rework, at cruise power, since the upper classes need the 4 kW rating that would spend the charge in three centuries, so that they enter dormancy at the end of the window rather than at the start. The expected value of a late release is the product of the pass probability, dormant survival, and the launch, braking, and settlement terms. At the cruise survival figure it is a few times 10^-4 per seed: a pass within 0.5 pc is near-certain over 500,000 years, survival at 0.99 per 665 years over that wait is ~5 × 10^-4, and the three per-leg terms are ~0.7. That figure does not apply to a buried package, and the true number is the on-ramp's to measure, not to assume. The mass is the binding constraint: five reference-class packages are ~18 tonnes delivered at the tens of km/s a rendezvous with a hyperbolic body costs from the belt, and the four seeds will be lighter than the reference seed only to the degree the resident can supply, from the body, what they would otherwise carry.

**Vigil, succession, and reach.** Three provisions turn the late release from a lottery ticket into a planned event, and two of the three are cheap in mass. The first is succession: each of the five carries its own charge, so a standby seed can take over as resident when the resident's is spent, five charges with one charge each giving ~40,000 years of sensing rather than 8,000. It costs a release per succession, since a seed's charge is what carries it to a star, and it is the fallback, not the plan. The second is a vigil. The energy in one charge is 3.8 × 10^13 J: 8,000 years at 150 W, but 120,000 at 10 W and 1.2 million at 1 W, longer than the interval between stellar passes. A watt is enough for a clock, a periodic integrity check of the ledger, and a trigger, and the question is not energy but hardware: a reactor cannot idle at a watt and would cycle, running at kilowatts for days per century, on control mechanisms no one can yet age-rate. The alternative is a vigil battery with no moving parts, a long-lived alpha emitter; the isotopes below lie outside the short-lived set Parisi (2026) proposes to breed with fusion neutrons, and their production at these masses is a reactor-blanket problem in its own right. At 5% thermoelectric conversion, americium-243 (half-life 7,400 years) gives a watt from ~3 kg, needs no criticality control, and covers the sensing window; uranium-233 (159,000 years) gives a watt from ~70 kg, about five bare critical masses (Paxton & Pruvost 1987), and covers one pass interval; neptunium-237 (2.1 million years) gives a watt from ~1 tonne, fast-fissionable at a bare critical mass of 57 ± 4 kg (Sanchez et al. 2008) and so some fifteen of them, and covers ten. Either long-lived source must be held subcritical by dilution and geometry, with its criticality safety designed together with the seed's own charge a few metres away. The masses are parent alpha energies at an RTG-class efficiency; the daughter chains, growing in over 10^4 to 10^5 years, raise the two long-lived sources' output several-fold, so they are conservative, and at ~20 W thermal the converter rather than the isotope may set the mass. Both long-lived isotopes are reactor products, which settlement zero's reactors make and a seed cannot in quantity; neptunium-237 arises at well under a kilogram per tonne of spent fuel, so a tonne of it is a scale marker, not a by-product, and U-233 bred from thorium is the practical vigil battery. The third is reach, and it matters most. A seed's reach to a passing star is set by how long it can cruise, and passes come as the square of the radius. On the Gaia rate (Bailer-Jones et al. 2018) a pass within 0.5 pc comes every ~200,000 years, within 1 pc every ~50,000, within 2 pc every ~12,500; the transit at 30 km/s is ~16,000, ~33,000, and ~65,000 years; the extra fuel at the 50 W profile is ~11, ~22, and ~45 kg. Survival at the cruise figure over wait plus transit, times the launch, braking, and settlement terms, gives an expected value per seed of ~0.03 at 0.5 pc, ~0.2 at 1 pc, and ~0.2 at 2 pc, falling again at 3 pc as the transit lengthens. Twenty to forty-five kilograms of fuel per seed, about 1% of its mass, raises the expected value from ~0.03 for a seed that can reach only 0.5 pc to ~0.2, a factor of about seven; the remaining gap to the few times 10^-4 of the dead-resident paragraph above is the encounter rate, the Gaia 200,000 years rather than the computed 500,000, which this paragraph adopts. Four seeds then give of order 0.8 expected settlements per rider, comparable per rider, at five times the mass, to an ordinary dispatch from settlement zero's launcher. Three cautions belong with that number: the survival figure is the cruise figure extrapolated over 10^5 years, which is unmeasured and, for a buried package and a 30 km/s transit, conservative; a release at 1 to 2 pc needs delta-v of tens of km/s to match the star's motion, on propellant from the body; and the released seed still faces the ordinary braking and settlement terms. The provisioning this paper recommends is three charges for the resident, and for each seed its settlement charge plus 22 to 45 kg of cruise fuel, two to four charges in all, sized for a 1 to 2 pc reach, and a vigil battery: about 250 kg of U-235 per rider, and a vigil battery the supply track of Section 3 must make. Every number here reproduces from the rider functions in engineering.py.

**Fuel.** That provisioning raises the question the bootstrapping paper leaves in principle: whether the fissile material can be mined where the lineage is. In the belt it cannot, not as U-235. CI chondrite carries ~8 ppb uranium and ~30 ppb thorium (Lodders 2003), a few hundred times poorer than Earth's continental crust (Rudnick & Gao 2003) and a hundred thousand times poorer than a typical ore body at ~0.1% uranium, and only 0.72% of the uranium is U-235. One seed charge of 16.5 kg lies in ~3 × 10^11 kg of rock, a body ~650 m across at 2 g/cm³; a provisioned rider's 250 kg in ~4 × 10^12 kg, a body ~1.6 km across; and grinding that much rock at 10 kWh per tonne costs ~1.6 × 10^17 J, ten years of a 500 MW plant's entire output, a plant that, if fission-powered, burns of order a tonne of U-235 a year, before leaching and before enrichment from 0.7% to the high enrichment a compact reactor needs, the fissile bottleneck the bootstrapping paper places at L4 to L5. The concentration is not the barrier: uranium is recovered from seawater at ~3 ppb with adsorbent chemistry (Abney et al. 2017). Dissolving a couple of cubic kilometres of rock is. Breeding changes the arithmetic by nearly three orders of magnitude. Thorium and U-238 together are ~40 ppb of the same rock and all of it is usable once bred to U-233 or Pu-239 in a reactor blanket, against 0.06 ppb of U-235, a yield per tonne nearly seven hundred times higher; 250 kg of bred fissile comes from ~7 × 10^9 kg of rock, a body under 200 m across. This is the bootstrapping paper's position restated with numbers: fissile closure begins late in L4 and completes at L5, and the route is breeding from fertile material, never mining U-235. For the home-system era the consequence is the supply track of Section 3: settlement zero, its seeds, and its riders are provisioned from Earth and the Moon, and every later settlement mines fertile, not fissile. Two caveats. No asteroid has been assayed for uranium in situ, so the ppb figures are meteorite and returned-sample averages, Ryugu's being CI-like (Yokoyama et al. 2023), that the prospector census would replace; and the seven-hundredfold advantage assumes the blanket and its reprocessing chemistry close in situ and a breeding gain above one, so that at doubling times of decades the pace is set by time and the starting inventory, not by rock, which is the L5 problem restated, not solved.

**What it crosses, and when.** The ethics paper places the lineage's irreversible beginning at the heliopause, and a rider crosses it about twenty years after placement. A body at 26 km/s could in principle be overtaken by anything settlement zero launches for as long as settlement zero can launch, and it is worth being explicit that the lineage does not do this. Recall is not an option by design, for the rider as for any seed: a lineage that retrieves what it has sent has made every dispatch provisional and every immutable core negotiable after the fact, and the ethics paper's one-way door is a door precisely because nothing comes back through it. The heliopause criterion therefore stands as written, and the consequence is exact. Placing a rider that carries reproduction-capable seeds on an interstellar object is an irreversible act, by policy at placement and by physics at the crossing, and the twenty years between are not a recall window, because no recall exists. It is S5 in the ethics paper's sense whether or not it is a dispatch in the routing paper's, and it waits on the same standing, the same core, and the same governance as the first seed from settlement zero's launcher; it is not an experiment settlement zero may run at S3 or S4 to see what happens. The roadmap paper's ordering makes settlement zero's first launch the intended S5 event; a rider so equipped placed before it would itself be that event. What a rider that cannot reproduce may carry, and when, is Section 8's question. Until placement the governance the roadmap paper sets out for the orphaned root applies in full, and the rider's take is among the smallest the lineage will ever make.

## 7. Recurring Comets as Platforms

The interstellar object passes once. A Jupiter-family comet comes back. That single difference makes the periodic comet the rehearsal the rider needs and a platform in its own right.

Jupiter-family comets are the ecliptic comets with Tisserand parameter between 2 and 3 (Duncan et al. 2004), which in practice have periods under twenty years and aphelia near Jupiter; of order a few hundred are known with perihelia inside 2.5 AU. Rosetta was designed to reach one, 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko, with a period of 6.4 years, a perihelion of 1.24 AU, and an aphelion near Jupiter's distance, after a ten-year cruise with four gravity assists (Glassmeier et al. 2007), and did, orbiting it for two years and landing Philae on it (Taylor et al. 2017). From the lunar platform the same rendezvous costs a few km/s and no decade.

A rider on a periodic comet can be serviced and upgraded at every perihelion, which no interstellar rider can be, and that changes what it carries. Because it is serviced, it can carry heavier and more complex instruments than an interstellar rider, and its job, like the scouts', is detailing our own solar system rather than the sky: an assay suite that characterises the comet's own volatiles and every body it passes as a fuel source, feeding the supply track's census; imaging of planets, moons, and small bodies from geometries and, at times, distances no Earth-orbit telescope has, including close passes on an orbit that runs from inside 1 AU to Jupiter's distance; and in-situ sampling of the interplanetary environment along that orbit. Deep-sky astrometry is not its purpose, and the deep-time navigation problem the consistency review names belongs to purpose-built observatories; the rider's long baseline is at most an opportunistic by-product.

Its second job is rehearsal. Everything the interstellar rider must do on a body it will never see again, anchoring, burial below the active layer, drawing propellant from the ice, running a resident on a reactor, holding seeds dormant and repairing them, and reporting on a beacon, a comet rider does on a body that returns every six years to be inspected. The comet is the harder host in one respect that makes it the better test: it is active. A Jupiter-family nucleus loses of order a metre of surface per perihelion averaged over its surface, and several metres and more in its most active regions, as 67P did in its southern hemisphere (Keller et al. 2015; Pätzold et al. 2019), so a rider on it must be buried beneath the layer that sublimates or must choose a dormant or extinct nucleus, and learning which is a result the interstellar rider, on a body that may itself be active near a star, needs.

Its third role is the one the consistency review lists as a candidate paper: the moving resource base. A comet is a volatile-rich body that carries a rider from inside Earth's orbit to Jupiter's and back on a schedule, which is a mobile depot and a mobile laboratory at once, and the first instance of a platform the series has so far only named, and the one that stays. What it cannot do is leave the solar system, and that is the limit that separates it from Section 6: a comet rider is a home-system asset for as long as the comet lasts, and it crosses no door.

## 8. The First Probes Outward

The programme's hard question is whether anything leaves the solar system before settlement zero exists, and the answer this series has already fixed makes the question exact. Section 6 has said why recall is not an option, and the consequence here is exact: a rider placed from the lunar platform before settlement zero exists crosses the heliopause about twenty years later, an irreversible act taken decades early, under the least tested core the programme will ever have, by Earth's governance rather than the settlement's. Section 6 says a rider carrying reproduction-capable seeds is not an experiment settlement zero may run at S3 or S4 to see what happens. This paper's position is that a few early riders are worth sending anyway, as test platforms for the issues nothing else can test, confined to the early phase, and that the reconciliation with no recall lies in what they carry.

Why send them at all. The roadmap paper's validation partition puts four things beyond the solar system's reach: cruise survival, dormancy and self-repair over millennia, magnetic-sail braking, and the immutable core under drift. An early rider is a testbed for the first two and, because it runs with no one able to intervene, for autonomy unsupervised, which the roadmap paper tests only under audit; it does not test braking. It leaves at 26 km/s with a resident running on a reactor, held packages dormant beside it, and a beacon toward the Moon, and it tests the first decades and then centuries of ageing, dormancy, cold restart, and unsupervised operation in real time, in the real medium, while the programme is still running and can act on what it learns. Nothing on the ladder does that. The roadmap paper's Section 9 says the untestable set stays untestable until a child arrives; the early rider moves the first centuries of it into the programme's own lifetime.

What makes it a test platform and not the lineage's first act. The ethics paper's threshold, read as this paper reads it, attaches to the capacity to reproduce, not to any vehicle: what must not leave the heliopause before the standing to launch has been exercised is a seed that can found a lineage. An early rider therefore carries a resident, instruments, a ledger, and a beacon, and it carries the seeds' hardware, four dormant packages whose ageing and whose repair by the resident are the point of the test, but those packages are inert: no fissile charge, and therefore no braking, no cruise, and no plant power, with the plant itself carried inert because its ageing is what the resident's repair test is for. The resident's own charge is fixed to the resident, and no succession is fitted, so no charge can migrate to a package. Four, to match the mass and layout of the provisioned rider; two are repaired by the resident through the window and two are left as controls, which makes the test an experiment. They are dormancy testbeds in the shape of seeds. A rider so equipped that leaves the solar system is a vehicle beyond recall, but it is not a lineage beyond recall, because it cannot found one; and the autonomous agent it carries beyond reach for eight thousand years is a D1-class node with no manufacturing and no launcher, so the security paper's blast radius is bounded by construction. The seeds that can found a lineage, provisioned as Section 6 recommends, ride only after S5, from settlement zero, under the standing the ethics paper describes. This is a refinement the ethics paper would have to accept, and it is stated here so that paper can reject it: the one-way door is the door through which reproduction passes, and an observatory that will never reproduce passes a lesser one. If it is accepted, two sibling sentences acquire the word reproduction-capable: the ethics paper's threshold, "once the first vehicle leaves the solar system," and the roadmap paper's "settlement zero is the last step that can still be recalled."

Confined to the early phase. Early riders are the programme's, not the lineage's, and the programme ends. After S5, riders are the settlement's to place, on the settlement's standing, with reproduction-capable seeds, and the early riders become what the roadmap paper's orphaned-root rule makes every rider: nodes for as long as their reactors last and archives after. The number is set by opportunity, not policy: the platform can reach of order one interstellar object a decade, so the early phase places a few riders, not a fleet, and each is an experiment whose result the next one uses.

## 9. Governance of the Earth Era

Three things the programme builds are dangerous, and the series' governance was written for none of them as they exist in the Earth era, before there is a settlement to bind.

The fissile chain. A lunar mine and breeder producing hundreds of kilograms of weapons-usable material, and a depot in the belt holding a tonne of it, are the ethics paper's dual-use problem in its most concrete form and the reason that paper's manufacturing-scope constraint exists. Section 3's rule is that fissile accounting is continuous and ledgered from mine to seed; the mechanism is the DNA mission-ledger paper's, applied before there is a mission, and the roadmap paper's information clause, that nothing reaches a seed except as a governed and ledgered update, applies to the depot first. The governance paper has since adopted that clause for a settlement's relationship to its own builders. In the Earth era the builders are the only party there is, and the clause is the whole of the governance until settlement zero can hold its own key.

The platform. A driver on the Moon that puts eighteen tonnes to 30 km/s is, without any change of hardware, a weapon that can deliver of order 10^13 J, roughly two kilotonnes of explosive equivalent, to any point in the inner solar system, and it can do so as often as its energy store can be recharged, daily on a square kilometre of array. The security paper's root-capture window, in which the lineage is a single node inside the civilisation that built it and containment has nothing to bound, has a second target here, and arguably the more attractive one, since a platform has no ledger, no core, and no autonomy to subvert. Nothing in this series governs a platform; the platform's governance is the polity's, and the programme should say so rather than pretend the ledger reaches it.

The founding key. The DNA mission-ledger paper replicates the founding public key across archive fragments and says nothing about who holds the signing key. Before settlement zero exists that holder is an authority, because there is no lineage to hold it otherwise. Whoever holds it in the Earth era writes the core the early riders carry and the core settlement zero will be delivered with. The ethics paper's consent argument, that the smaller and less representative the deciding body the more conservative the core must be, binds hardest here, at the moment the body is smallest, and it is the argument for Section 8's rule: nothing that can reproduce leaves until the standing has been exercised by a process the ethics paper would recognise as defensible, documented, and contestable.

## 10. Ordering and Parallelism

The tracks run in parallel and the ladder in sequence, and the programme's critical path is not the ladder. It is the lunar driver and the breeder.

What starts now: the S0 inventory and the S1 plant, which the roadmap paper already places first; a lunar assay of the KREEP terrane and the polar ice, on the ground and beyond the orbital thorium maps; the siting of the lunar driver; and the first scouts to the belt on conventional launchers, ahead of the platform. Within a decade: the S2 process demonstrator on the Moon, which the supply track has now given a reason to be there; the first lunar driver segment at the Ames scale and its first scouts; the mine. Within two decades: S3, the near-Earth seed; the breeder and the first banked fissile material; depots cached in the belt; the full lunar driver at its 30 km/s length; the first comet rider, and, when an interstellar object comes within reach, the first early rider, as Section 8 defines it. Within three to four decades: S4 on the roadmap paper's schedule, provisioned from the bank rather than from its own closure, settlement zero delivered beside a depot, and, at the end of that span, S5, the first reproduction-capable dispatch, on the standing Section 9 describes.

Two features of the ordering matter more than the dates. The tracks shorten the ladder, because S4 no longer waits on its own fissile closure to provision seeds. And the platform outlives the window: the subsystem budget paper measures the home programme's political will in decades and expects it to fade, and a programme whose product is a driver on the Moon, a bank of fuel in the belt, and a census in the ledger has left the lineage what it needs even if the will fades before S5.

## 11. Failure Modes

- **Capture of the fuel chain.** A breeder producing weapons-usable material by the hundred kilograms is diverted, or the depot is claimed. The ledger records it and prevents nothing; the defence is the polity's, and the programme should not claim otherwise.
- **The platform repurposed.** The lunar driver is the programme's most durable asset and its most dangerous, and the two facts are the same fact. A platform that outlives its builders' purpose outlives their restraint.
- **Scouting that never becomes a census.** A fleet that images bodies and assays none, or assays them without the ledgered record the replication model needs, produces science and not a census. The measure of the scouting track is bodies characterised to the standard a settlement can plan on.
- **A depot that corrupts the measurement.** Feedstock cached beside the S4 seed is drawn on before the closure levels it would mask are measured, and the vitamin fraction the ladder exists to measure is lost in the one place it mattered.
- **An early rider that reproduces.** A test platform placed with a charge that could brake and settle, or with a plant, has founded a lineage under the S3-era core by accident, and no recall means by accident is forever.
- **A core revised after the first rider.** Even an observatory rider carries the core, and a core revised before S5 leaves the early riders carrying a different one, which is the speciation paper's divergence problem beginning before the lineage does. The programme should treat the early riders' core as the S5 core's first draft and version it accordingly.
- **The window closes early.** The political will fades before the breeder or the lunar driver is finished, and the programme leaves a mine without a launcher or a launcher without fuel. Ordering the critical path first, and banking fuel from the start, is the only hedge.

## 12. Relationship to Other Papers

This paper is the roadmap paper's supply side and its first act. It takes that paper's ladder, its rung labels, its no-resupply rule, its prospector definition, its orphaned-root rule, and its launcher sizing as given, and it takes over from that paper's second revision the supply track, the interstellar-object rider, and the fuel budget, which are reproduced here with their references and their code. The roadmap paper keeps a pointer in each place.

It puts numbers to the bootstrapping paper's fissile bottleneck and confirms that paper's placement of fissile closure at L4 to L5 by showing that it is a breeder problem; it adds one item to that paper's know-how rather than its vitamin set, helium-3 extraction, under a fusion assumption stated and isolated in Section 3. It takes the subsystem budget paper's decades-long home window and answers it with a platform that outlives the window. It takes the ethics paper's standing argument and consent constraint and proposes a refinement of that paper's heliopause threshold, that irreversibility attaches to the capacity to reproduce, which that paper must accept or reject; if accepted, that paper's "once the first vehicle leaves the solar system" and the roadmap paper's "the last step that can still be recalled" both acquire the word reproduction-capable. It extends the governance paper's home-system clause to the depot, and finds the series silent on the platform, which it leaves to the polity. It gives the security paper's root-capture window a second target. It takes the knowledge-growth paper's yardstick as the reason a rider is a node and not a vault. It gives the consistency review's moving-resource-base candidate its first instance in the comet rider, and the deep-time navigation candidate a boundary: astrometry belongs to observatories, not to scouts. And it reads the Fermi paper's messenger probe in reverse, as something this programme places rather than something it looks for.

## 13. Conclusion

The lineage's first generation leaves on Moon-bred fuel and belt-mined structure from a settlement Earth delivered, and everything in that sentence except the settlement is a programme that can start now. Fuel, because chondritic rock cannot supply it and the Moon can. A platform, because the Moon's well is shallow and a driver there is a railway, not a country. A census, because no body beyond Earth has been assayed and the replication model needs the assay. And a few probes outward early, because the one thing the solar system cannot validate, deep-time survival with no one listening, can only be tested by sending something to survive it, and a rider that cannot reproduce may go where a seed may not. The programme's limits are governance and time. The physics is the easy part, which in this series is the unusual finding.
