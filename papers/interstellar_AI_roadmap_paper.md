# Settlement Zero: A Validation Roadmap for the Slow Lineage, from Near-Earth Demonstrator to First Dispatch

**S. Stone**
*Working draft, revision 2 (revised after a line-level review and a cross-paper audit against all sixteen siblings). Seventeenth in a series on a slow, self-replicating interstellar AI probe. The series' consistency review names the missing on-ramp directly: "No paper addresses how the architecture could be incrementally tested — precursor missions, in-solar-system demonstrators, what is prototypable now versus what waits on high-closure manufacturing. The series is all destination, no on-ramp." This paper supplies the on-ramp, and argues that its last rung is not a test at all but the lineage's root.*

---

## Abstract

Every paper in this series describes what a self-replicating interstellar seed must do at a star it will not reach for millennia. None describes how any of it could be tested before launch. This paper supplies that roadmap and draws one conclusion from it. The architecture partitions cleanly into what the solar system can validate and what it cannot: industrial closure, the bootstrapping cascade, the vitamin fraction, autonomous manufacturing, sub-probe operations, and the launcher are all testable on asteroidal material within decades, while cruise survival, magnetic-sail braking against the interstellar medium, millennia of dormancy, and the immutable core under drift are not. We organise the testable part as a six-rung readiness ladder, S0 through S5, from the capability inventory that exists today (sample return from Bennu and Ryugu, Dawn at Ceres, Psyche in cruise, in-situ oxygen production on Mars, a ground-tested kilowatt fission reactor, a flight-tested autonomous planner) through a terrestrial closed-loop plant, an off-Earth process demonstrator, a near-Earth asteroid seed operating under a strict no-resupply rule, and a main-belt settlement. The ladder's central discipline is the no-resupply rule: a demonstrator that may be resupplied from Earth measures nothing, because the quantity the series most needs measured, the vitamin fraction, is by definition what a settlement cannot make. The paper's central claim is the settlement-zero argument. The vehicle paper assumes a "home-system" launch of the first seed and a settlement-built launch of every later one, and treats the two as analogous. They should be identical. The main-belt settlement that closes the ladder is the root node of the lineage, not a rehearsal for it: it has the mass base (~2.4 × 10^21 kg), it sits in a power regime far closer to a settlement at another star than any near-Earth site, it must build the same ~4 × 10^14 J launcher, its own existence carries none of the ~0.63 per-leg discount every later node is born under, and its production lifetime is bounded only by policy, so it can launch far more than the three offspring the reproduction knife-edge demands. Generation zero is also the only generation humans will ever watch complete a reproduction cycle, which makes it the sole direct measurement of the manufacture and launch terms the R_eff model otherwise assumes. We name the sub-probe variant the settlement builds for the resource census the *prospector*, place its first flight at the near-Earth rung, and show why the census is necessary rather than optional: the ore-bearing fraction of near-Earth objects is of order one in a thousand. We size the launcher for the first time and find it the largest structure in the architecture. We close with what the on-ramp cannot reach, including a correction to the braking model's density that the on-ramp surfaces, and with the on-ramp's own failure modes, of which resupply creep is the most likely.

---

## 1. The On-Ramp the Series Lacks

The series has a destination and no road to it. The vehicle paper fixes the cruise regime at ~450 km/s, and the analytical engineering paper closes the braking geometry at d = m/(2ρA). The bootstrapping paper renders "what the probe must be able to do" as a five-level closure ladder, L1 through L5. The subsystem budget paper prices a ~3,700 kg reference seed to the component and finds the manufacturing plant at 54% of it. The computational engineering paper turns per-stage reliabilities of ~0.9 into a reproduction knife-edge, R_eff = 0.48 / 0.94 / 1.39 / 1.85 for one to four offspring, and the routing paper turns R_eff into a dispatch policy. Each of these results rests on numbers that are estimates: the ~3% vitamin fraction, the ~0.9 manufacture-stage survival, the time to climb from a landed seed to launch-capable settlement. No paper says how any of them would be measured.

This is a defect the series' own method makes acute. The overriding rule is realistic physical constraint, and a claim that cannot in principle be tested before the mission that depends on it is a weaker kind of claim than the earlier papers usually make. The 1980 NASA summer study that gave the self-replicating factory its first engineering treatment understood this: its lunar-factory chapter was framed as a twenty-year demonstration program, not a launch (Freitas & Gilbreath 1982). Metzger et al. (2013) later argued that an in-space industrial base could be bootstrapped from a small landed kit through a sequence of increasingly capable generations, each built by the last, with terrestrial resupply shrinking toward zero. That is the bootstrapping paper's cascade, proposed for the solar system rather than for another star, and it is the natural on-ramp for this series.

This paper does three things. It partitions the architecture into what the solar system can validate and what it cannot (Section 2), states the one rule that makes validation meaningful (Section 3), and lays out a six-rung readiness ladder from the present capability inventory to the first interstellar dispatch (Section 4). It then argues that the ladder's final rung is not a demonstrator but the lineage's root, and that treating it as such removes a discontinuity the vehicle paper has carried since its first revision (Section 5). It names and places the sub-probe variant the settlement will need for its resource census (Section 6), sizes the launcher structure, whose energy the vehicle paper gives but whose length, power, and g-load no paper has (Section 7), lists what the on-ramp would measure (Section 8), states honestly what it cannot (Section 9), and closes with the on-ramp's own failure modes (Section 10).

## 2. The Validation Partition

The architecture splits into two sets, and the split is clean.

The solar system can validate everything that happens *after* a seed is on a small body:

- mining, refining, and forming from asteroidal feedstock;
- the bootstrapping cascade from simple tools to capable ones, and the closure fraction reached at each rung of L1–L5;
- the vitamin fraction, measured rather than estimated;
- autonomous manufacturing without a human quality organisation;
- the two-mode thermal system, cruise radiators carried and manufacturing radiators built in situ;
- sub-probe construction and operation;
- the fixed launch infrastructure;
- the reproduction cycle end to end, from arrived seed to launched child.

This set contains the manufacturing plant, which is 54% of the seed and the binding constraint everywhere in the architecture. It also contains, at its top, the manufacture and launch terms of the per-leg survival product.

The solar system cannot validate anything that depends on interstellar distance or deep time: cruise survival at 0.99 per light-year against interstellar dust and cosmic rays over thousands of years; magnetic-sail braking against the interstellar medium, with the correction developed in Section 9; dormancy and self-repair over millennia; the stability of the immutable core and the drift of the interpretive layer over the timescales the speciation paper models; the lineage-network paper's heartbeat and fork reconciliation at real light-delay; and R_eff itself, which is a property of a population of settlements and not of any one.

The partition has a consequence the rest of the paper develops. The testable set is the set the earlier papers say is hardest *as engineering*: closure, the plant, the launcher. The untestable set divides in two. Its physical members, gentle dust, speed-independent braking, patience, are the ones the physics already argues for. Its one non-physical member, the core under drift, is the governed-amendment paper's problem, bracketed there and bracketed here: no on-ramp reaches it. The on-ramp therefore tests the right things, and what it leaves untested is either favoured by physics or already declared open.

## 3. The No-Resupply Rule

A demonstrator in the solar system has a resource no interstellar seed will have: Earth is reachable. Anything that breaks can be replaced, any process that fails can be supplemented, any vitamin that runs short can be sent. This is what makes solar-system testing possible and what makes it worthless if it is not disciplined.

The quantity the series most needs measured is the vitamin fraction: the mass of parts a settlement cannot make and must carry. The computational engineering paper's point estimate for a mature settlement is ~3%, ~30 kg per tonne. The bootstrapping paper, as its own contribution, traces the trajectory down to that floor from a first-landed fraction that Borgue and Hein (2021) put at roughly 30% of probe mass for a near-term design. Both are estimates throughout. A demonstrator measures the fraction only if it is forbidden from importing anything after landing, because the vitamin fraction is, by definition, whatever the demonstrator would have imported. A demonstrator that may be resupplied cannot distinguish "closed" from "supplemented." Its closure fraction is a number about the supply chain, not the plant.

The rule is therefore: **from the moment a rung's seed is delivered, nothing further arrives.** The seed carries a declared inventory. Everything else it uses it makes. When a process fails, the seed repairs it from local material or does without. The measured outputs are the closure fraction reached at each L level, the mass and composition of the declared inventory that was actually consumed, the time to each level, and the list of what the seed needed and did not have. That last list is the vitamin set, observed.

Three corollaries follow. First, a demonstrator under the rule must be allowed to fail, and its failure is data; the rung is repeated with a revised inventory, and the revision is the vitamin trajectory being measured one step at a time. Second, the rule is easy to state and hard to keep, since the pressure to send one more part to rescue an expensive mission will be immense; Section 10 treats resupply creep as the on-ramp's likeliest failure. Third, the rule applies to information too, with one exception. A seed under test may receive new instructions from Earth exactly as an interstellar seed may receive them from the lineage network: as governed, ledgered updates to the mutable cognitive layer, never as an operator's hand on the controls. The DNA mission-ledger paper's audit machinery is the right instrument to enforce this, and running it on a demonstrator is itself a test. This scopes, rather than breaks, the vehicle paper's rule that the architecture survives with no communication back to Earth. That rule binds dispatched seeds. Settlement zero is inside the solar system and receives governed updates until the day it dispatches.

## 4. The Readiness Ladder, S0 to S5

We render the on-ramp as six rungs. Each states its site, its target closure level on the bootstrapping paper's L1–L5 ladder, what it measures, and roughly where it sits on the technology-readiness scale (Mankins 1995), used here as a shared vocabulary rather than a gate.

- **S0 — The capability inventory.** *Site: existing missions and facilities.* This is what has already been done, and the on-ramp begins by mapping it onto the architecture rather than by proposing anything. Small bodies have been visited, characterised, and sampled: Ryugu is a rubble pile of bulk density ~1.2 g/cm³ and more than 50% porosity (Watanabe et al. 2019), and the ~120 g returned from Bennu is a serpentine-dominated, carbon- and nitrogen-rich, phosphate-bearing regolith (Lauretta et al. 2024), which is what a carbonaceous feedstock will actually look like to a landed plant. Dawn showed Ceres to be a volatile-rich body with a strong lithosphere over a weaker mantle (Russell et al. 2016), almost certainly the belt's largest reservoir of water. Psyche is en route to test whether a metal-rich body is a core fragment or unmelted metal-rich material (Elkins-Tanton et al. 2022), the belt's clearest candidate metal feedstock.
  In-situ resource use has left the laboratory: MOXIE produced oxygen from the Martian atmosphere by solid-oxide electrolysis repeatedly across its first year (Hoffman et al. 2022), a single-process, single-product demonstration, and the first one. Kilowatt fission power has been ground-tested end to end in KRUSTY (Poston et al. 2020), a reactor of the kind the subsystem budget paper carries. Spacecraft autonomy has flown: the Remote Agent on Deep Space 1 ran model-based planning and closed-loop goal-directed commanding onboard (Muscettola et al. 1998). And self-replication of a limited kind exists on the bench, in printers that print most of their own parts (Jones et al. 2011). None of this is a closed plant. All of it is TRL 6 to 9 for the individual pieces the plant will need.

- **S1 — The terrestrial closed-loop plant.** *Site: Earth, in vacuum and on simulant. Target: L2, with L3 attempted.* The first rung is a factory-in-a-chamber under the no-resupply rule: a declared inventory and a stock of asteroid simulant matched to the Bennu and Ryugu returns, and nothing else enters. The plant must climb from regolith to bulk structure and mechanical parts (L2) and attempt optics and instrument bulk (L3), running the subsystem budget paper's P1–P4 process stages, P5 being an L5 matter, without a human quality organisation. Ellery (2016) argues that printing actuators and control electronics from regolith-derived feedstock would be an existence proof of universal construction; S1 is where that proof is attempted. This rung is cheap, repeatable, and the only one where the vitamin set can be revised in weeks rather than years. It is also where the bootstrapping paper's metrology bottleneck first bites: the plant must hold tolerance against references it made itself. TRL 4 to 5 for the integrated plant, which has no heritage even though its pieces do.

- **S2 — The off-Earth single-body demonstrator.** *Site: the Moon or a near-Earth asteroid. Target: L2.* The plant's core processes, not the whole plant, on real material under real gravity, thermal cycling, dust, and radiation, still under the rule. This is MOXIE scaled from one process to the P1–P3 chain: excavate, beneficiate, reduce, form. The Moon is the easier site and the wrong feedstock; a near-Earth asteroid is the right feedstock and the harder site. Both are worth doing, and the choice between them is programmatic rather than architectural. TRL 6 for the process chain.

- **S3 — The near-Earth asteroid seed.** *Site: a near-Earth asteroid in the few-km/s accessibility class (Sanchez & McInnes 2011). Target: L3, with L4 begun.* The first full seed. It is delivered once, carries a declared inventory sized by S1 and S2, and climbs the ladder alone. Its targets are a settlement that manufactures its own instruments (L3), begins its own power and radiator structure (L4), and builds and flies its first sub-probes (Section 6). This is the rung at which the two-mode thermal architecture is first exercised as designed: the carried ~11 m² cruise radiators against the hundreds to thousands of m² of manufacturing radiators the settlement must build. It is also the first rung at which the D1 degraded state of the knowledge-growth paper, sub-probe capable but not launch capable, is a real operating mode rather than a contingency, since S3 is not expected to launch anything interstellar. TRL 7 for the plant.

- **S4 — Settlement zero.** *Site: the main belt. Target: L4 complete, L5 attempted.* A seed in the ~3,700 kg reference class, delivered to a belt body or a co-located pair of bodies giving both metal and volatiles, under the rule, with the target of closing the power and launch infrastructure level. Settlement zero must grow the megawatt-class manufacturing power the analytical engineering paper describes, from reactors built in situ with arrays as supplement, as the subsystem budget paper specifies, at 14% of the sunlight available at Earth. It must build the launcher (Section 7). It must run the prospector census across its neighbourhood. And it must attempt L5, closing microelectronics, fissiles, and metrology, knowing that the bootstrapping paper calls L5 the research frontier and that a settlement stalled at L4 with a small vitamin set is still a settlement. TRL 8 for the plant; the launcher's readiness is set here.

- **S5 — First dispatch.** *Site: settlement zero. Target: a reproduction cycle completed.* Settlement zero manufactures a child seed, a mobile bootstrap package rather than a copy of itself, loads it with the vitamin set S4 measured, and launches it at cruise speed toward a first target. Then it launches another, and another. This is not a test that ends. It is the lineage's first generation, and Section 5 argues that this is the only honest way to describe it.

The ladder is sequential in principle and overlapping in practice. S1 runs continuously alongside everything above it. S2 and S3 can be concurrent if the programme can afford both. S4 does not wait for S3 to finish, only for S3 to have measured the inventory S4 must carry. The rungs are ordered by what each one needs to know, not by what each one costs.

Durations are programmatic, and we state them only to make "within decades" honest. S1 is a laboratory programme measured in years, and it never ends. S2 and S3 are mission-class undertakings of about a decade each from decision to result, the span Dawn and OSIRIS-REx took from selection to return. S4 is the long pole: the 1980 study's twenty-year programme for a lunar factory (Freitas & Gilbreath 1982) is the only prior estimate of its class, and the belt adds years of transit and setup. Two to four decades from S3 to first dispatch is the order of magnitude, and the ratchet time S3 measures is what would tighten it.

## 5. The Settlement-Zero Argument

The vehicle paper says that launch impulse should be supplied by fixed infrastructure, "home-system at first, and locally rebuilt at each settlement thereafter," and that launching a child "mirrors the first probe's departure from Earth." The subsystem budget paper repeats the mirror: the settlement's launcher sends children onward "exactly as Earth's launch infrastructure provides the departure impulse for the original probe." Both treat the first launch and every later launch as *analogous*. This paper's claim is that they should be *the same event*, performed by the same kind of node, and that the belt settlement at the top of the ladder is that node.

Five arguments support this, in increasing order of weight.

**The belt buys mass, not delta-v, and mass is what the seed needs.** The Sun's gravity well is nearly irrelevant to a 450 km/s cruise. Escape from a heliocentric circular orbit at 1 AU costs ~12 km/s; from 2.7 AU, ~7.5 km/s. A 450 km/s hyperbolic excess needs a launch speed of ~452 km/s at Earth's distance and ~451 km/s at the belt's, and since a body at 1 AU is already moving at ~30 km/s while one at 2.7 AU moves at ~18, the belt is in fact ~11 km/s *worse* on delta-v. The claim is literally true: the belt buys nothing in velocity. What it offers instead is ~2.4 × 10^21 kg of small-body material (Pitjeva & Pitjev 2018), over a third of it in Ceres alone, fourteen to eighteen orders of magnitude more than any seed, which is the analytical engineering paper's reason that materials closure never binds. Launching from the belt means the seed's manufacture and its launcher's manufacture both draw on that reservoir, and Earth lifts only the first seed.

**Settlement zero faces a power regime close to the one every later settlement will face.** Sunlight at 2.7 AU is ~190 W/m², 14% of the value at 1 AU. A settlement there cannot live on the arrays a lunar base would use. It must do what the subsystem budget paper says every settlement must do: run cognition and survival on a kilowatt-class reactor and grow manufacturing power from reactors and collectors it builds. A lunar or near-Earth demonstrator does not test that regime. Settlement zero does, and the knowledge-growth paper's nominal environment for any settlement is exactly this one: "a planetary system with an asteroid belt," with the settlement sited in the inner belt for metals, silicates, stable dynamics, and adequate solar energy. Where a settlement at another star actually sits depends on the host's luminosity and where its small bodies are, so the match is to a regime, not a number; but it is far closer than any site nearer the Sun offers.

**The launcher is the same problem, and it need only be solved once.** Sending a ~3,700 kg seed to ~450 km/s requires ~3.75 × 10^14 J of kinetic energy plus losses, whether the launcher stands at Earth, in the belt, or at Proxima. Section 7 shows how large that launcher is. A programme that builds it at the belt, from belt material, with settlement zero's own plant, has demonstrated the L4 closure the bootstrapping paper says every reproducing settlement must reach. A programme that builds it at Earth from terrestrial industry has demonstrated a launch. Only one of these is evidence for the lineage.

**Settlement zero can launch far more than the knife-edge demands, and it should.** The computational engineering paper needs three or more offspring per node at per-stage reliabilities near 0.9, or higher reliabilities at fewer, to escape extinction. Three things distinguish settlement zero from every node after it. Its own existence carries no discount: every later node exists only with the ~0.63 per-leg probability of having survived manufacture, launch, cruise, braking, and settlement, while settlement zero, delivered by a conventional mission and grown under observation, exists with probability one. It sits on the largest resource base any node of the lineage will ever have. And its production lifetime is bounded only by policy: the engineering papers make a finite production lifetime a divisor on offspring count "wherever a node's production lifetime is finite," and the vehicle paper allows persistent production "for as long as local material and unclaimed targets last," so a node whose material is effectively unlimited and whose targets are the whole catalogue has no natural end to its dispatch programme. The subsystem budget paper already assumes a multi-probe home programme, "tens of probes at intervals of years," but frames its window as political, sustained while "political and economic will" lasts and then fading. Settlement zero relocates that window from the polity to the settlement. The routing paper's coverage-first policy, directing each dispatch toward the target with fewest prior attempts, is one every node executes; what settlement zero uniquely brings is the offspring count to execute it in depth from the start, placing three independent attempts on each articulation point and high-viability host within 20 ly in the first generation rather than over the first several. The routing paper charges each extra offspring a full seed mass and a delay to the reproduction cycle, and that charge stands; at settlement zero it is paid in belt material under human observation, which is the cheapest place the lineage will ever pay it. Two caveats belong here. The routing simulation gives the Sun the same offspring count as every other node, so a root dispatching a dozen seeds has not been simulated, and its effect on the 62,000-year coverage-first advantage is an extension the routing paper should run. And a root of that degree changes the early topology the lineage-network paper describes, from a chain of two, then three nodes into a star, which shortens the tree-to-mesh timeline and makes settlement zero the network's dominant single point of failure until cousins reconnect.

**Generation zero is the only generation humans will ever watch complete.** The per-leg survival product in R_eff multiplies ~0.9 at manufacture, ~0.9 at launch, ~0.9 at braking, and ~0.9 at settlement, with 0.99 per light-year of cruise. The braking and settlement terms cannot be observed until a child arrives somewhere, ~2,800 years after the first dispatch to Proxima. The manufacture and launch terms can be observed at settlement zero, repeatedly, by the people who built it. If settlement zero attempts twelve seeds and launches ten successfully, the manufacture-and-launch product is ~0.83, measured, and the R_eff model has two of its four assumed factors replaced, as a product, by data. The measurement carries a caveat: it is data for node zero, which has S1 to S3 heritage, governed updates from Earth, and the belt's resource base, and is not automatically the per-stage constant for a node at another star. But no other event in the lineage's history is observable by its founders at all. This is why S5 is the correct end of a *validation* roadmap despite being the start of the mission: it is the last measurement anyone on Earth will take.

The consequence for the series is small in wording and large in structure. The phrase "as with the first probe leaving Earth" should read "as with the first seed leaving settlement zero." The Earth launch of the reference seed at 450 km/s, whose energy the vehicle paper gives as ~10^14 to 10^15 J and whose structure no paper has sized, disappears. What Earth launches is a ~3,700 kg seed to a belt body at a few km/s of delta-v beyond escape, a mission class within reach of flown capability. Everything after that is the lineage doing what the lineage does. One distinction should be kept exact. The ethics paper places the lineage's irreversible beginning at the moment "the first vehicle leaves the solar system," and that threshold survives: settlement zero is the lineage's root node, but the lineage is *begun*, in the ethics paper's sense, at S5, when the first seed leaves it. Settlement zero is the last step that can still be recalled.

## 6. Sub-Probes and the Prospector

The payload paper and the knowledge-growth paper already use the term *sub-probe* for the vehicles a settlement builds and flies within its own system: landers and sampling craft for the in-situ science that remote sensing cannot do, and the survey fleet of the D1 degraded state. We keep the term as the class name and add one member.

A **prospector** is a sub-probe built for the resource census: the survey of a system's small bodies for the composition, accessibility, and mass the vehicle paper's replication model requires before a settlement can plan a child. Its instruments are the L3 products of the settlement, spectrometers and imagers on locally made optics and structure, with a carried detector core. Its propulsion is solar-electric or, in the outer system, reactor-electric, with delta-v in the few-km/s class that moves between neighbouring small bodies; a short mass driver of a few kilometres, which at 1,000 g delivers ~8 km/s, is the natural departure impulse (Section 7). It returns samples to the settlement rather than to Earth. Its mission ends by being recycled.

The census is not optional, and the reason is quantitative. Elvis (2014) estimates that the fraction of near-Earth objects that are ore-bearing in any commercial sense is of order one in a thousand or worse: roughly one in 2,000 for platinum-group metals, one in 1,100 for water. Those thresholds are set by terrestrial economics and are not the settlement's thresholds, since a settlement needs bulk metal and volatiles rather than platinum, and it needs them within reach rather than at a profit. But the underlying point transfers: composition varies enormously between bodies, the fraction suitable for any given purpose is small, and a settlement that commits to the wrong body has wasted its intra-system transfer and years of ratchet time. Lewis (1996) made the general case that the small-body population contains everything a space-based industry needs; Elvis's arithmetic says that finding the specific bodies that contain it is work. The prospector is that work, and the settlement cannot reproduce until the work is done.

The prospector's first flight belongs at S3, the near-Earth seed, for two reasons. It is the first vehicle the seed manufactures rather than carries, and so is the first test of L3 as a *product* rather than a capability. And its survey of the seed's own neighbourhood is the settlement's first observational knowledge in the knowledge-growth paper's sense, from which its first operational knowledge, a bootstrap plan refined by what is actually there, is derived; that is the kind the lineage-network paper says propagates horizontally between nodes once the mesh matures. At S4 the prospector fleet becomes the instrument by which settlement zero selects the bodies its children's launchers and factories draw on, and by which the belt is mapped at a resolution no Earth-based survey will reach. At S5 and after, the prospector is the D1 fallback: a settlement that loses its launcher keeps its prospectors, and keeps learning.

Four terms are worth fixing here so that later papers do not drift. A *seed* is the mobile bootstrap package delivered to a body it will settle, at whatever distance: Earth delivers one to the belt, and settlement zero launches them to other stars. A *child* is a seed launched by a settlement to another star. A *sub-probe* is anything a settlement builds to operate within its own system. A *prospector* is the sub-probe variant for the resource census. A prospector never leaves its system; a child never returns to it.

## 7. The Launcher, Sized for the First Time

The series has named the launcher three ways (mass driver, beamed-energy array, powered solar Oberth pass) and has sized none of them; the vehicle paper gives the energy and stops. The roadmap cannot avoid the structure, because settlement zero must build one and S5 must use it. What follows is first order and meant to fix the scale, not the design.

The energy is fixed by the seed. A 3,700 kg seed at 450 km/s carries 3.75 × 10^14 J, about 100 GWh. Energy is not the problem. A 10 km² photovoltaic array at 2.7 AU, at 25% efficiency, delivers ~470 MW and accumulates that energy in about nine days; at a few hundred grams to a kilogram per square metre such an array masses ~3 × 10^6 to 10^7 kg, thousands of seed masses, which is a settlement-built structure of Freitas-factory scale rather than a panel. The subsystem budget paper's baseline is the other route, fission plants built in situ with collectors as supplement, and either delivers the energy within weeks. The problem is delivering it to the seed.

**A mass driver** must be long. Length is L = v²/(2a). At 1,000 g the driver is ~10,000 km long and the launch lasts ~46 s; at 10,000 g it is ~1,000 km and ~5 s. The seed, its reactor, its shadow shield, and its plant must survive that acceleration, and 10,000 g is well beyond what a reactor with moving conversion machinery tolerates. Mass is no obstacle to a 1,000 km structure; capability is, and its straightness, alignment, and power switching are engineering the series has not touched. The driver must also be anchored: a launch delivers ~1.7 × 10^9 N·s of recoil, nothing to a body of 10^20 kg and a great deal to a free-floating structure, which fixes the driver's site to a large body and makes the prospector census a launcher-siting survey as well.

**A beamed array** must be powerful and must hold focus over a long distance. Reflecting light off a sail gives thrust F = 2P/c. To reach 450 km/s over 1 AU the seed needs ~0.7 m/s² for ~8 days, a thrust of ~2,500 N, and therefore ~380 GW of delivered optical power, ~2.5 × 10^17 J in total, larger than the seed's kinetic energy by the factor c/v ≈ 670, which is the price of transferring momentum with photons. That is four Starshot-class arrays running for a week, with focus held across 1 AU on a sail attached to a multi-tonne payload, and it is the reason the vehicle paper's gram-scale beamed sail does not scale to the seed.

**A solar Oberth pass** is an alternative for a slow seed, not a stage for a fast one. A drop from the belt to a 0.1 AU perihelion costs ~13 km/s at the belt, less than the ~17 km/s from Earth orbit, though at the ~0.3 AU perihelion Maraqten et al. (2026) study the two sites cost the same. The perihelion burn then pays out at high leverage: Maraqten et al. find that high-temperature photovoltaics powering an electric burn there can send ton-class payloads to ~40 km/s of excess speed. But the leverage exists only for a seed that arrives at perihelion slowly. Reaching 450 km/s by Oberth alone would need a perihelion burn of order 300 km/s, and a seed that a driver has already brought to hundreds of km/s gains from a perihelion burn little more than the burn itself. The three launchers are alternatives; they do not stack. The Oberth route reaches tens of km/s, and the other ~400 is the driver's or the array's.

The honest summary is that, at first order, the launcher is likely the largest single structure in the architecture, larger than the settlement's factory, and that the mass driver is the only one of the three that scales down for testing: a driver of a few kilometres can launch prospectors and demonstrate the switching, alignment, anchoring, and payload-survival questions at S3 before settlement zero builds the full length. This is the strongest reason to place the launcher on the roadmap rather than leave it to a settlement 2,800 years away. It is also the point at which we should say plainly that the 450 km/s cruise has a cost the earlier papers have not counted, and that a slower cruise may be what settlement zero actually builds first. At 250 km/s the driver is 31% of the length and dust energy falls by the same factor, and the vehicle paper's gravitational-navigation criterion, which wants excess speed no larger than about the escape speed at periapsis, is easier to meet. Against that, cruise fuel and cruise survival are per-time quantities, not per-distance ones: at 250 km/s a light-year takes ~1,200 years instead of ~665, the ~1.4 kg/ly fuel figure becomes ~2.5 kg/ly, 0.99 per light-year becomes ~0.98, and the ~12 ly standard hop range on a fixed charge falls toward ~7 ly, which moves the routing paper's reachability fractions. The vehicle paper's regime is "a few hundred km/s," and the roadmap is where the exact number gets decided by what can be built and what the network can afford.

## 8. What the On-Ramp Measures

The value of the ladder is the list of canonical numbers it converts from estimates to measurements. In order of when they arrive:

- **The closure fraction at each level (S1 onward).** The bootstrapping paper's L1–L5 ladder is a set of milestones with a closure fraction each; S1 measures L2 and L3 on simulant, S2 and S3 on real feedstock.
- **The vitamin set and its mass (S1 onward).** Observed, under the no-resupply rule, as the declared inventory consumed and the list of what was needed and absent. The ~3% floor and the trajectory down to it become data.
- **The ratchet time (S3 and S4).** The time from landed seed to each closure level, which no paper has estimated, and which sets the production-lifetime term in the engineering papers' replication budget.
- **The two-mode thermal design (S3).** The carried ~11 m² cruise radiator against the manufacturing radiators built in situ, as the subsystem budget paper specifies.
- **Autonomous quality (S1 onward).** Whether in-process monitoring can mature into closed-loop control without a human qualification step, which the bootstrapping paper identifies as the binding condition at seed scale.
- **The ledger under independent audit (S3 onward).** The DNA mission-ledger paper notes that in the lineage "the audited party and the auditor are the same system." The on-ramp is the only regime in which they differ: humans audit the seed's ledger and attempt to tamper with it, and the log is the proof that the seed's autonomy held.
- **The manufacture and launch terms of per-leg survival (S5).** The only two of the four ~0.9 factors that will ever be observed by the lineage's founders.
- **The launcher's actual scale and the cruise speed it sets (S4, S5).** Which decides, for the whole series, whether 450 km/s is the number.

Two numbers the ladder does *not* measure deserve naming, since a reader might expect them: R_eff, which needs a population, and the braking term, which needs an arrival.

## 9. What the On-Ramp Cannot Reach

Four things stay untested until a child arrives, and one of them carries a correction the earlier papers should record.

**Cruise survival** at 0.99 per light-year is an assumption about dust, radiation, and component ageing over ~665 years per light-year. The solar system offers accelerated ageing on the ground and a few decades of flight heritage, and nothing at the timescale. The vehicle paper's argument that slow cruise is gentle, ~4,400 times less dust energy than at 0.1c, is physics and not measurement, and it stays that way.

**Dormancy and self-repair over millennia** are the same problem from the inside. The vehicle paper's five-class repair spectrum can be exercised at S3 and S4 against faults that occur in decades. Faults that occur in centuries are not in the test.

**The immutable core under drift** is the payload paper's and the speciation paper's problem. A settlement operating for decades under ledger audit and governed updates to its cognitive layer is a test of the audit mechanism, not of the core's endurance, and governed amendment of the core itself stays bracketed.

**Magnetic-sail braking** carries the correction. The braking model in the analytical engineering paper, the computational engineering paper, and the vehicle paper uses ρ ≈ 3.3 × 10^-22 kg/m³ for the local interstellar medium, from n ≈ 0.2 cm^-3 (Redfield & Linsky 2008; Frisch et al. 2011), and d = m/(2ρA) is independent of cruise speed. That n is the local cloud's *neutral* hydrogen density. Its electron density is ~0.07 cm^-3, so hydrogen there is only about a quarter to a third ionised (Slavin & Frisch 2008; Frisch et al. 2011). A magnetic sail couples to charged particles: the momentum-braking treatments the vehicle paper relies on model protons reflected from the magnetosphere (Gros 2017). The mass the sail directly sweeps is therefore roughly a third of the model's ρ, and unless charge exchange couples the neutrals at high efficiency, stopping distance and stopping time both grow by about a factor of three: a tonne seed's ~7 years over ~0.005 ly becomes ~20 years over ~0.015 ly, and the reference seed's ~25 years over ~0.02 ly becomes ~70 years over ~0.06 ly. Braking stays feasible; the numbers move, and the three papers should carry the ion-only ρ or state the factor. What the solar system can do about this is specific. The solar wind is a fully ionised plasma at ~400 km/s relative velocity, coincidentally the cruise speed, with a mass density of ~1 × 10^-20 kg/m³ at 1 AU falling to ~1 × 10^-21 kg/m³ at 2.7 AU, within a factor of a few of the interstellar value. A sail deployed at settlement zero therefore sees nearly flight-like plasma conditions, and the coupling physics, the magnetosphere's effective area A against a flowing plasma, can be measured there. What it cannot measure is the neutral coupling, because the solar wind has none. Whether the local cloud's neutral fraction couples through charge exchange, and at what efficiency, is a question the roadmap surfaces and cannot answer.

## 10. Failure Modes of the On-Ramp

The roadmap has its own ways to fail, and they are mostly programmatic rather than physical.

- **Resupply creep.** The likeliest. A seed under the rule stalls at L3 for want of one component, the component costs nothing to send, and the mission is expensive. Sending it converts a measurement into an anecdote. The defence is to write the rule into the mission's authority structure before launch and to treat a stalled seed as a completed experiment with a result.
- **Simulant that is not feedstock.** S1 measures closure on a material that is, at best, a mineralogical match to the Bennu and Ryugu returns. Porosity, grain structure, volatile content, and the electrostatic behaviour of real regolith in vacuum are not in the simulant. S1 results are a lower bound on difficulty and should be reported as such.
- **The human in the loop.** A demonstrator that reaches L3 with operators diagnosing faults from Earth has not demonstrated autonomy. The rule's information clause, governed updates only and never a hand on the controls, has to be enforced by the ledger, and the ledger's log is the proof.
- **The wrong body.** A plant validated on a metal-rich body and landed by the first child on a carbonaceous one, or the reverse, has been validated for a different feedstock. The prospector census exists to prevent this at the target; at S3 and S4 the programme must deliberately test both feedstock classes.
- **Capture.** A belt settlement at L4 is an industrial asset of a kind that has never existed, and the pressure to use it for something other than launching seeds will be at least as strong as the pressure to resupply it. This is a threat the security paper's adversary model does not cover: it arises inside the launching civilisation, not from another lineage, and it targets the one node through which every first-generation route passes, the articulation point of the whole early network. The ethics paper's question of who has standing to launch the lineage becomes concrete at S5, and the constraints the ethics paper derives for the immutable core have to be in place at settlement zero before it has anything worth capturing. The governance paper's E0–E7 ladder has no rung for a settlement's relationship to its own builders; the information clause of Section 3 is the rule that fills it, and the ladder's bound-the-take rule should be checked against a launcher of a thousand kilometres and a dozen seeds.
- **The launcher decides the cruise speed, and no one notices.** If settlement zero builds a shorter driver and the first generation leaves at 250 km/s, every transit time in the series lengthens by a factor of ~1.8, and, because fuel and survival per light-year are per-time quantities, hop range shrinks and catalogue reachability changes with it (Section 7). That is acceptable; the architecture is speed-independent in braking and robust in dust. It is not acceptable for it to happen without the series' numbers being re-derived.

## 11. Relationship to Other Papers

This paper adds no new subsystem and no new mechanism. It supplies the sequence in which the existing ones would be built and tested, and it draws one structural conclusion.

It takes its closure ladder from the bootstrapping paper and its process stages from the subsystem budget paper, and proposes to measure both. It takes the launcher's energy from the vehicle paper and sizes its structure, finding it the largest in the architecture and the strongest reason a slower cruise may be chosen; the vehicle paper's "few hundred km/s" regime accommodates that. It takes R_eff and the per-stage reliabilities from the computational engineering paper and identifies the two factors that can be observed, and the production-lifetime term from the engineering papers and identifies the node it does not bind. It takes the dispatch policy from the routing paper and gives it a root that can execute it in depth, at the cost of an n_root extension that paper's simulation does not yet have. It takes the sub-probe from the payload and knowledge-growth papers, the D1 state from the latter, and adds the prospector. It takes the no-resupply rule's information clause from the DNA mission-ledger paper's audit machinery and gives that paper its only independent auditor. It hands the ethics paper a date and a place: standing to launch is exercised at S5, from settlement zero, and settlement zero must be governed before it is capable. It gives the lineage-network paper a root of high degree rather than a chain, and the security paper an inside threat its model does not cover. It gives the Fermi paper a correction of phrase: "a single launch from a home system" becomes a single settlement in a home system. And it gives the three papers that carry the braking model a density correction.

The settlement-zero argument revises the vehicle paper's framing without contradicting its physics. Where that paper has "home-system at first, and locally rebuilt at each settlement thereafter," this paper says the home-system launch *is* a settlement launch, and that the phrase "as with the first probe leaving Earth" describes an event that should not occur. What leaves Earth is a seed bound for the belt. What leaves the belt is the lineage.

## 12. Conclusion

The series has argued for a slow, self-repairing, self-replicating probe on the strength of physical constraint, and has left its central engineering numbers as estimates. This paper shows that the numbers that matter most, closure, the vitamin fraction, the ratchet time, the manufacture and launch reliabilities, and the launcher's scale, are all measurable inside the solar system within decades, on asteroidal material, by a sequence of demonstrators disciplined by a single rule: nothing arrives after the seed. The numbers that cannot be measured are the ones the physics already favours, and the one open problem the on-ramp cannot reach was open already.

The sequence ends at a main-belt settlement that builds a launcher and launches seeds. Calling that a demonstrator would be a mistake. It is the lineage's root, with the largest resource base and the most offspring any node will ever have, in the power regime later settlements will recognise, and it is the only generation whose reproduction anyone on Earth will see. The vehicle paper's first probe does not leave Earth for another star. It leaves Earth for the belt, and the belt does the rest.
